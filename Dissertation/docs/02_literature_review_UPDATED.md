## 2.1 Overview

This chapter reviews the literature underpinning the dissertation in five stages. Section 2.2 defines concept drift and its taxonomy, establishing the distinction between changes in the input distribution and changes in the input–output relationship. Section 2.3 reviews existing drift detection methods and identifies their central limitation: they are predominantly reactive, signalling drift only after predictive performance has degraded, or they monitor input distributions that may remain stationary while the underlying relationship changes. Section 2.4 reviews variable importance and model explanation methods, which offer a window into how a model uses its features. Section 2.5 examines the small but growing body of work at the intersection of explainability and drift detection, and Section 2.6 introduces Rashomon sets and Model Class Reliance as an unexplored detection mechanism. Section 2.7 covers the benchmark datasets and evaluation methodology used in this field. The chapter concludes by stating the research gap and the research questions that follow from it.

## 2.2 Concept drift: definitions and taxonomy

Supervised learning conventionally assumes that training and deployment data are drawn from the same joint distribution P(X, y). In many business settings this assumption fails: customer preferences shift, economic conditions change, and adversaries adapt. The resulting phenomenon, in which the joint distribution changes over time, is termed concept drift (Widmer and Kubat, 1996; Gama et al., 2014).

The literature decomposes the joint distribution as P(X, y) = P(X) · P(y | X), and this decomposition structures the standard taxonomy. A change in P(X) alone, with P(y | X) unchanged, is termed virtual drift (or covariate shift); a change in P(y | X) is termed real concept drift, since it alters the decision boundary the model must learn (Gama et al., 2014; Webb et al., 2016). Lu et al. (2019) note that the two frequently co-occur in real data, which complicates attribution of any observed change to one component. This distinction is central to the present dissertation: the detection problem is hardest when P(y | X) changes while P(X) remains stationary, because no amount of monitoring the inputs alone can reveal the change.

Drift is further characterised by its temporal profile. Gama et al. (2014) distinguish sudden (abrupt) drift, in which the concept switches at a point in time; gradual drift, in which instances from the old and new concepts are interleaved over a transition period; incremental drift, in which the concept moves smoothly through intermediate states; and recurring drift, in which previously seen concepts return, as with seasonal effects. Webb et al. (2016) formalise these profiles quantitatively, arguing that qualitative labels such as "gradual" conceal important differences in drift magnitude and duration. The temporal profile matters for detector design because a detector tuned to abrupt changes may accumulate evidence too slowly to identify incremental movement, and vice versa (Ditzler et al., 2015).

The practical consequence of drift is model degradation: a model trained on historical data becomes progressively miscalibrated with respect to current data, and business decisions based on its outputs deteriorate accordingly (Žliobaitė, Pechenizkiy and Gama, 2016; Bayram, Ahmed and Kassler, 2022). The operational question is therefore when to retrain, which converts the scientific problem of drift detection into a monitoring-cost problem: retraining too often wastes resources and destabilises downstream processes, while retraining too late incurs the cost of degraded predictions. Detection methods that provide earlier warning directly reduce the second cost.

## 2.3 Drift detection methods

Detection methods divide broadly into those that monitor the model's error stream and those that monitor the data distribution. Both families are reviewed here because they supply the baselines against which this dissertation's proposed signals are evaluated, and because their limitations define the research gap.

### 2.3.1 Error-based detection

The most widely used detectors monitor a supervised performance signal. The Drift Detection Method (DDM) models the classifier's error rate as a binomial variable and raises a warning when the rate exceeds its historical minimum by a threshold number of standard deviations (Gama et al., 2004). The Early Drift Detection Method (EDDM) extends this by monitoring the distance between classification errors rather than the error rate itself, improving sensitivity to gradual drift (Baena-García et al., 2006). ADWIN maintains a variable-length window over the error stream and cuts the window whenever two sub-windows exhibit statistically distinguishable means, with guarantees on false-positive and false-negative rates (Bifet and Gavaldà, 2007). The Page–Hinkley test, a sequential change-point method originating in statistical quality control, cumulates deviations of the error signal from its running mean and signals when the cumulative deviation exceeds a threshold (Page, 1954). Further variants apply statistical tests over chunked error streams (Nishida and Yamauchi, 2007) or Hoeffding-bound-based comparisons (Frías-Blanco et al., 2015).

These methods share two structural limitations. First, they are reactive by construction: the error rate can only rise after the model has begun making mistakes on the new concept, so detection necessarily lags the onset of drift (Ditzler et al., 2015; Lu et al., 2019). Second, they require timely ground-truth labels to compute the error stream, an assumption often violated in deployment, where labels arrive with delay or not at all (Sethi and Kantardzic, 2017). Both limitations motivate signals derived from the model itself rather than from its verified errors.

### 2.3.2 Distribution-based and model-internal detection

A second family avoids the label requirement by monitoring the input distribution P(X) directly. Approaches include incremental two-sample tests such as the sequential Kolmogorov–Smirnov test of dos Reis et al. (2016) and discriminative approaches such as D3, which trains an auxiliary classifier to distinguish old from new windows and signals drift when it succeeds (Gözüaçık et al., 2019). These methods detect covariate shift effectively, but they are blind by construction to real concept drift under stationary P(X): if only P(y | X) moves, the inputs carry no signature of the change (Webb et al., 2016).

A smaller line of work monitors internal properties of the deployed model rather than its inputs or verified errors. Sethi and Kantardzic (2017) track the density of predictions falling within the classifier's margin, arguing that a rise in margin density indicates growing uncertainty and can precede measurable error increase without requiring labels. This work establishes a precedent for the present dissertation's central premise: signals extracted from how the model processes data can move before the error rate does. However, margin density is a scalar summary; it identifies that something has changed but not which features are implicated, limiting its diagnostic value for practitioners deciding how to respond.

## 2.4 Variable importance and model explanation

Variable importance methods quantify how much a model relies on each feature, and therefore offer a richer, feature-resolved view of model behaviour than scalar signals such as accuracy or margin density.

Breiman (2001a) introduces permutation importance alongside the Random Forest algorithm: the importance of a feature is the degradation in predictive performance when that feature's values are randomly permuted, breaking its association with the target. Permutation importance is model-agnostic and intuitive, but it inherits known biases, including inflation for correlated predictors and for variables with many categories (Strobl et al., 2007).

Lundberg and Lee (2017) unify several attribution methods under SHAP (SHapley Additive exPlanations), which assigns each feature a contribution to each individual prediction based on Shapley values from cooperative game theory. SHAP possesses desirable axiomatic properties (local accuracy, missingness and consistency), and the TreeSHAP algorithm makes exact computation tractable for tree ensembles, enabling local explanations to be aggregated into global importance profiles (Lundberg et al., 2020). Covert, Lundberg and Lee (2020) extend the Shapley framework to global importance directly with SAGE, which attributes the model's predictive performance, rather than its individual predictions, across features. The distinction between explaining predictions and explaining performance is material to this dissertation: attribution over predictions describes what a fixed model does, whereas attribution over loss describes how well the model's use of each feature matches the current environment.

A critical caveat runs through this literature: explanation methods explain the model, not the world (Rudin, 2019; Molnar, 2020). A SHAP profile computed on a frozen model is a deterministic function of that model and the inputs it receives. It follows that if the input distribution is stationary, the importance profile of a frozen model remains stationary regardless of how P(y | X) moves — the explanation faithfully describes a model that is now wrong. This observation, developed further in Chapter 3, implies that naive importance monitoring on a static deployed model cannot detect pure concept drift, and that importance-based detection must be designed with care over what is explained (predictions versus loss) and which model is explained (frozen versus retrained).

## 2.5 Importance-based drift detection: an emerging intersection

A small body of work applies model explanation to the drift problem directly. Demšar and Bosnić (2018) compute streaming model explanations and detect drift as change in the explanation stream, demonstrating on benchmark streams that explanation-based signals can identify drift and, in some configurations, localise it to specific features. Haug et al. (2022) detect change in local feature attributions over evolving data streams, and incremental variants of global importance measures have been proposed to make Shapley-based monitoring computationally feasible online (Muschalik et al., 2023). Collectively this work establishes feasibility: importance signals can be computed in streaming settings and do respond to drift.

Three gaps remain. First, the early-warning question is largely unaddressed: existing studies ask whether importance-based detection works, not whether it fires earlier than error-based detection, and lead time relative to performance degradation is rarely reported as a primary metric. Second, the frozen-versus-retrained distinction identified in Section 2.4 is generally implicit rather than analysed: studies that recompute explanations on periodically retrained models conflate the drift signal with the retraining schedule, while studies on static models are structurally limited under stationary inputs. Third, no work to date uses the Rashomon set or Model Class Reliance, reviewed next, as the monitored object.

## 2.6 Rashomon sets and Model Class Reliance

Breiman (2001b) observes that many distinct models frequently achieve near-identical predictive accuracy on the same data, a phenomenon he terms the Rashomon effect. The set of near-optimal models — the Rashomon set — can contain models that rely on substantially different features while performing equally well (Fisher, Rudin and Dominici, 2019; Semenova, Rudin and Parr, 2022).

Fisher, Rudin and Dominici (2019) formalise reliance across the Rashomon set as Model Class Reliance (MCR): for each feature, MCR reports the minimum and maximum reliance on that feature over all models in the set. Where single-model importance describes one arbitrary member of the set, MCR bounds describe the entire class of adequate explanations of the data.

This dissertation proposes MCR as a drift-detection object on the following logic. Under a stable concept, the Rashomon set and its reliance bounds are properties of the data-generating process and should themselves be stable across time windows. When P(y | X) moves, the set of near-optimal models changes: models that were adequate exit the set, differently structured models enter it, and the MCR bounds shift accordingly. A shift in which models are adequate may plausibly precede a visible fall in the deployed model's accuracy, because the deployed model can remain marginally acceptable while the composition of the set around it changes. No published work has tested this mechanism, which constitutes the most novel component of the present dissertation.

## 2.7 Benchmark data and evaluation methodology

Empirical drift research relies heavily on synthetic streams with known drift points, because ground truth for drift onset is otherwise unobservable. The rotating hyperplane generator produces pure real concept drift by rotating a linear decision boundary while the input distribution remains uniform and stationary (Hulten, Spencer and Domingos, 2001); the SEA generator produces abrupt drift by shifting a threshold on a sum of features (Street and Kim, 2001). Losing, Hammer and Wersing (2016) assemble these and other synthetic and real streams into a benchmark suite used across the literature, and open-source frameworks such as MOA (Bifet et al., 2010) and River (Montiel et al., 2021) provide reference implementations of the detectors reviewed in Section 2.3.

The rotating hyperplane stream is methodologically important for this dissertation: because P(X) is stationary by construction, any detection achieved on it cannot be attributed to covariate shift, isolating the hard case identified in Section 2.2. Conversely, SEA-style threshold drift provides a contrast case in which the set of relevant features does not change, testing whether importance-based signals fail where feature relevance is preserved.

Evaluation in this field uses three primary quantities: detection delay (time from true drift onset to detection), false-alarm rate (detections raised under a stable concept), and, for the early-warning question specifically, lead time relative to the point at which predictive performance measurably degrades (Gama, Sebastião and Rodrigues, 2013; Bifet and Gavaldà, 2007). Sound evaluation further requires prequential (test-then-train) assessment so that performance estimates are not contaminated by data the model has already seen (Gama, Sebastião and Rodrigues, 2013).

## 2.8 Synthesis and research gap

The reviewed literature supports the following argument. Concept drift degrades deployed models, and the operationally valuable moment to act is before accuracy visibly falls (Section 2.2). Existing detectors either wait for errors to accumulate, and are therefore reactive and label-dependent, or monitor input distributions and are therefore blind to real concept drift under stationary inputs (Section 2.3). Variable importance methods provide a feature-resolved signal of model behaviour (Section 2.4), and early work confirms such signals respond to drift in streaming settings (Section 2.5) — but that work has not established whether importance signals lead performance degradation, has not disentangled frozen-model from retrained-model signals, and has not exploited the Rashomon set, whose composition may shift before any single deployed model visibly fails (Section 2.6).

The dissertation addresses this gap through three research questions:

- **RQ1.** When real concept drift occurs, do variable-importance signals shift in a detectable way?
- **RQ2.** Do these signals shift earlier than predictive performance degrades — that is, do they provide useful early-warning lead time?
- **RQ3.** How do SHAP-based, ensemble vote-dominance and MCR-based mechanisms compare against error-based baselines on detection delay, false-alarm rate and lead time?
